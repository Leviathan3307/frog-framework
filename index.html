What this guide does

It helps you turn the things you’ve done into checked notes you can reuse for applications and interviews.

The whole method in four lines

  1. Say where you’re heading. Even “not sure yet” is a start.
  2. List your experiences. Short headings, each with an ID that never changes (E01, E02 and so on).
  3. Let an AI chat interview you, one question at a time, about one experience.
  4. Check every claim against what you actually said, then approve the record.

After that, the same records feed role research, applications and interview practice, so you stop re-remembering your own history every time.

What you walk away with: an evidence bank

A set of notes on your experiences, each checked against what you actually remember.

Meet Bear

Bear is the fictional applicant you’ll follow through every stage. Two of Bear’s experiences get the full treatment, so you can see the method work on very different material.

Bear is a postgraduate with an earlier analyst job and a recent group project at university. Bear is weighing up operations, business analysis and consulting, and opens with this:

“I have some analyst experience and a group project, but I don’t know which things are worth mentioning.”

Most people start here. The experiences exist, but on a CV they shrink to one line each, and those lines don’t tell an interviewer what you actually decided or did.

E01
Work example, carries most of the guide

“Helped automate a monthly reporting process.”

E02
University example, shorter but complete

“Completed a university group project analysing several companies.”

Short Bear notes run through the instructions. The full story, start to finish, is the case study in Part 4 (page 22).

Why keep an evidence bank at all?

An evidence bank is a set of notes on your experiences, each checked against what you actually remember.

Every application asks you to reflect again: a CV bullet, a cover letter, a motivation answer, a competency question. Without a record, you rebuild the story from memory each time and it drifts, usually towards sounding bigger than it was.

A record lets you do the hard remembering once, check it once and reuse it everywhere. It also keeps the honest limits attached, so an estimate stays an estimate when you’re nervous in an interview.

No analyst job? No problem.

Bear’s E02 is a university assignment with no number attached, and it still makes a strong story. Coursework, internships, part-time jobs, volunteering and society roles all work.

You don’t need a performance number. You need a moment where you made a decision.

Bear is fictional

Bear isn’t a real person or test participant. The stories are invented illustrations based on how this method works in practice. Nothing in them is a measured result of using the guide.

Before you start

Ten minutes of setup saves you rewriting the same story in three different chats.

What to have to hand

  • Your CV, if you want to use it. A few rough headings work just as well.
  • A role or two you might want. “Not sure yet” is a valid answer.
  • Somewhere to keep a few text files: a folder, a notes app or cloud storage. They’re plain text files ending in .md (Markdown), which any notes app or text editor opens.

Decide what stays private

Don’t paste confidential employer or client documents, private customer records, other people’s personal details or anything under a confidentiality agreement. Describe the decision and your part in it in general terms instead. Bear’s E01 uses synthetic (made-up, not real customer data) test cases for exactly this reason. Check your AI tool’s privacy and data settings before you start.

Which AI chat?

Any mainstream chat assistant can run these prompts. Some can create files you can download; many can only show text in the conversation.

Every prompt in this guide covers both cases. If the AI can’t make a real file, it returns the complete text and you save it yourself. If it says it has saved something and you can’t open or download it, treat it as not saved.

Attach the prompt pack to your first message so the AI uses the same file structure. Get it at leviathan3307.github.io/frog-framework/Frog_framework_prompt_pack.md, or paste its text into the chat.

Try talking instead of typing

If your AI has a voice mode, answering aloud often brings back detail that typing doesn’t. Read the transcript before you accept it, because speech-to-text gets names and numbers wrong.

Pick a direction

Say which roles you’re curious about, or admit you aren’t sure. Both give the AI something to work with.

The AI asks up to five questions, one at a time, then writes a short direction note split into what you’ve confirmed, what’s possible and what’s still open.

Prompt 1Pick a directionPaste into your AI chat

I’m using the Frog framework to explore my experiences before making career decisions. The goal is a short, honest direction note, not a career diagnosis. If I attach Career_bank.md or my CV, read them first, and treat the CV as incomplete context rather than proof of what I did. Ask me up to five questions, one at a time, about roles I’m considering, what interests me and any constraints I choose to share. I may be unsure, and that is a valid answer. Do not infer my personality or abilities from my degree, CV or job titles. Create or update Career_bank.md. If I attach the Frog templates, follow them. Otherwise keep these sections in this order: Version and Approval lines, Direction note (Confirmed, Possible, Open), Experience index, Next actions, Change history. Raise the version number each time the file changes. Show me the note and ask me to approve or correct it. If you cannot create or edit files, return the complete Career_bank.md as Markdown for me to save, and do not say it has been saved. Finish by suggesting the next step: listing my experiences.

Stage 3 of 4

Interview one experience

Pick one experience and let the AI question you until you can say exactly what you did, what you decided and what you can back up.

This is a conversation, not a form. The AI asks one focused question, waits and follows your answer. When something is vague, it asks for a specific moment. When you give a number, it asks where the number came from. It won’t write polished interview answers yet, because the evidence isn’t checked.

Prompt 3Interview one experiencePaste into your AI chat

I’m using Frog to explore one experience before deciding which career and interview stories it can support. Read the latest Career_bank.md, my CV if supplied and the latest record for [E##], if one exists. The experience I want to explore is [heading].

Start with what actually happened and what I personally did. Ask one focused question at a time, wait for my answer and probe the parts that remain vague. Follow the evidence, not a fixed STAR template. Ask about a particular decision or disagreement, the alternatives I saw, my responsibilities versus other people’s, what changed, what I can substantiate and what I still do not know. If I mention a number, ask what it measures, the period, the source and whether it is observed, reported or estimated. If I have no number, do not invent or require one. Let me say ‘skip’, ‘not sure’ or ‘pause’. If I correct something you said, follow my correction.

Create or update one detailed record named [E##]_short_name.md, marked Draft for review. Start it with Version, Approval and Privacy lines, following the record template if I attach it, and raise the version each time it changes. Keep the chronology, dates if known, my actions separately from others’, evidence strength, open questions and corrections. Update its status in Career_bank.md and show me the material changes. Do not write polished interview answers yet. If you cannot edit files, return the complete current Markdown for both files for me to save.

Why it matters
Most of the useful detail only appears on the second or third question.

Stage 4 of 4

Check every claim

The risky moment isn’t the conversation. It’s when a confident summary reads well and you approve it without noticing it says more than you did.

Before you approve a record, ask the AI to check it line by line. It has to find the exact answer behind each claim and put each one in a category. It should flag stretched wording itself, not wait for you to spot it.

Prompt 4Check every claimPaste into your AI chat

Audit the current [E##_short_name.md] line by line before I approve it. Read it alongside our conversation and any documents I supplied. For each ownership claim, outcome, figure, causal claim, tool and motivation, quote the exact answer or document that supports it. Classify each as directly supported, self-reported, estimated, interpretation or unsupported. Flag wording that makes a team result mine alone, turns an estimate into a realised result or widens the scope beyond what I described, even if I have not questioned it. Show the risky wording beside a narrower alternative and ask me to resolve each uncertainty. Do not quietly substitute your interpretation for my memory, and keep anything we cannot resolve visible as an open question. Show the full audit in the chat. In the record, keep only the corrected wording, open questions, a one-line change history entry and a short evidence list: one line per kept claim giving its category and where it came from, such as ‘my answer in this session’ with the date, or a document name and section. Return the updated draft. Mark it Approved, in the record and in Career_bank.md, only when I confirm. If you cannot edit files, return complete current Markdown for both files.

Why it matters
An inflated claim is worse than a modest one. It falls apart at the first follow-up question.

Stage 4, final step

Build an interview card

Only now, with an approved record, does the AI write answers. It adds them inside the same file so the evidence and the answer travel together.

The card is a short navigation section at the bottom of the record. It tells you which questions the story can answer, how to start it and where its limits are.

Prompt 5Build an interview cardPaste into your AI chat

First check the record’s Approval line. If it is not Approved, stop and tell me to finish Prompt 4 before building the card. Using my approved [E##_short_name.md], prepare a compact interview-navigation section inside that same record. Include a memory cue, four to six interview questions this evidence can genuinely answer, a natural 30-second summary, a natural 60-second answer, three to five likely follow-up probes and any source limits I must remember. Write the answers in plain spoken English I would actually say, not a polished script. Use only confirmed or explicitly qualified evidence, and do not force the story into a role or question where it does not fit. Show the new wording to me for approval, then update this record and the Career_bank.md index. If files cannot be updated, return complete current Markdown for both.

What goes in a card

  • Memory cue. Five or six words to find the story under pressure.
  • Questions it can answer. Interview questions this evidence honestly covers.
  • 30 and 60-second answers. In words you’d actually say.
  • Follow-ups. What a sharp interviewer will ask next.
  • Source limits. What you must not overstate.

Stage 4, any session

Save and pick up again

A new chat remembers nothing from the last one. Your files remember everything, as long as you take them with you.

End every session with a handover, even a short one. It forces the AI to give you the complete current files and to say what’s still unresolved. Start the next session with the resume prompt and the files attached.

Prompt 6End a session with a handoverPaste into your AI chat

We’re ending this session. Create a handover from the latest documents. List every file that changed, its current version and approval status, unresolved questions and the next recommended action. Ask me to check any material factual change before it is finalised. Then return the complete current Career_bank.md and every experience, role, application or interview file touched in this session. If you can create real downloadable files, do so and tell me exactly which ones exist. Otherwise supply complete Markdown under each filename and tell me which files to save. Do not assume the next chat will remember this one.

Prompt 7Resume in a new chatPaste into your AI chat

I’m continuing Frog framework work in a new chat. Read the attached latest Career_bank.md and [E##_short_name.md]. Do not assume you have any file I have not attached, or that you remember earlier chats. Summarise the experience’s current version and approval status, the last approved changes and the most useful next question, then ask it. Ask one question at a time. Keep confirmed facts unless I correct them, and mark new material as Draft for review. When I say ‘save this session’, prepare complete updated versions with changes and approval status. Create files only if your tools genuinely support it; otherwise return complete Markdown for me to save.

Attach to a new chat

  • Career_bank.md, always
  • The record you’re working on, such as E01_monthly_process.md
  • For A to D, the relevant note and the job description
  • Frog_framework_prompt_pack.md, so the AI keeps the same file structure
  • Your CV, only if you choose to
One current version

Replace the old file with the new one each time, rather than keeping both. If you do end up with two, ask the AI to compare them and list the differences, decide which is right and save one.

No file saving?

Copy the complete Markdown the AI returns into a text file named exactly as shown. Once per session is enough.

E01
Interview card

Monthly exception-processing improvement

Memory cue“Check the rules before making them faster”

Situation and decision

Each month a team worked through recurring exceptions with spreadsheet checks and database extracts. Before automating, Bear questioned whether the inherited rules still matched an updated category definition.

What Bear did

Compared the current procedure with the old logic; prepared three synthetic boundary cases; took them to the senior colleague; built and tested the revised routine; added an exception log for reviewers.

Others: Lead supported scoping and approval. The senior colleague agreed the revised rule. Others owned quality assurance and release sign-off.

Results and limits

Handed over for monthly use after testing. Team planning estimate: about 10 staff hours a month before, about 4 after, so around 6 a month. Estimated, not measured after release. No audited baseline.

About 30 seconds

“At a previous analyst job, I worked on a monthly process that involved a lot of repeat checking. When we considered automating it, I noticed that some of the old rules no longer matched an updated procedure. I brought a few test cases to the senior colleague who owned the original checks, and we agreed the revised logic. I built and tested the routine and added exception logging. What I learnt was to check whether a process still makes sense before making it faster.”

About 60 seconds

“In an analyst role, our team spent time every month working through recurring exceptions using spreadsheets and database extracts. My lead and I discussed automating the repetitive checks. As I compared the existing logic with the current procedure, I found a category change that the original checks did not fully cover. I made three synthetic test cases to show the difference and went through them with the senior colleague who maintained the old process. We agreed the correct handling before I wrote the revised routine. I tested the normal and boundary cases, added exception logging for the reviewers and handed it over for the team’s release checks. Our planning estimate suggested a reduction of around six staff hours per month, although we hadn’t measured consistent post-release savings. The useful lesson for me was that automation starts with understanding the process, not copying it.”

Questions it can answer

  • Improving a process
  • Challenging an assumption
  • Working with a more experienced stakeholder
  • Verifying an analytical conclusion
  • Balancing speed with traceability
  • Learning from an existing system

Likely follow-ups

  • How did you estimate the hours?
  • Who had final approval?
  • How did you test it?
  • What did you personally build?
  • What if the colleague still disagreed?

Still unresolved

  • Actual realised hours and error rates
  • Variation by month
  • Rule change versus scripting effect
  • Month and build time

For a pure teamwork question, E02 is the better choice.

E02
Interview card

Comparative company analysis

Memory cue“Agree what the numbers mean first”

Situation and decision

Four students compared four consumer-goods companies from public reports for a management module. Early ratios used different definitions and periods, so the first chart looked comparable but wasn’t.

What Bear did

Owned the shared comparison workbook; built a reference sheet with each ratio’s definition, source and financial period; asked teammates to check their inputs; analysed one assigned company.

Others: Each teammate analysed one company. The conclusion and presentation were developed jointly, and the team changed Bear’s over-absolute closing line.

Results and limits

Assignment completed and presented. Lecturer feedback: state the central conclusion earlier. No grade, client or business outcome recorded.

About 30 seconds

“In a four-person university project, we compared four consumer-goods companies. I built the common workbook and realised our initial ratios weren’t comparable because we had used different definitions and reporting periods. I made a reference sheet, checked the inputs with the others and completed my assigned company analysis. We then developed the conclusion together. It showed me how much the quality of a recommendation depends on agreeing what the numbers actually mean.”

About 60 seconds

“For a management assignment, my group of four had to compare the performance of several consumer-goods companies. We divided the initial research, but when we brought it together I spotted that our ratios used different reporting periods and definitions. I owned the common workbook, so I built a reference sheet recording each ratio’s formula and source, and worked with the others to make the comparisons consistent. I also completed the analysis for my assigned company. During the presentation preparation, the team challenged a conclusion I’d suggested because it sounded too absolute, so we changed the wording without losing the evidence. We delivered the presentation, and the lecturer’s main feedback was that we should have stated the conclusion earlier. I learnt to check comparability before drawing a conclusion, and to explain it in language the audience can use.”

Questions it can answer

  • Teamwork
  • Dealing with inconsistent evidence
  • Analytical problem-solving
  • Receiving feedback
  • Integrating parallel workstreams

  • Which formulas differed?

  • What changed after feedback?

When it goes wrong

The usual snags, and what to do about each one.

It made me sound more impressive than I was.
Run Prompt 4. It has to quote what you said behind each claim. That’s how Bear’s “led a company-wide programme” disappeared (page 28).
I can’t remember the details.
Say so. Bear couldn’t remember the month or the build time, and the record says “not remembered”. A gap is better than a guess.
It asks five questions at once.
Reply: “One question at a time, please, and wait for my answer.”
I have a number but I’m not sure it’s real.
Ask where it came from. If it’s a projection, label it as one, the way Bear’s six hours is a team estimate.
My story has no number at all.
That’s fine. E02 has none and it’s still a strong story. Don’t let the AI invent one.
The new chat has forgotten everything.
Normal. Attach Career_bank.md and your record, then use Prompt 7 (page 15).
It says it saved a file, but I can’t find it.
Only trust files you can open or download. Ask for the complete Markdown in the chat and save it yourself.
I’ve got two versions of the same record.
Attach both, ask the AI to list every difference, decide which is right and keep one current file.
My story doesn’t fit the question.
Pick a different story. The coverage table on page 17 shows how Bear chooses.

About this guide

One honest story is enough to start.

Frog comes from how I prepared for my own career applications. I used an extended form of this process to go back through my experiences, build a detailed story bank and prepare for several different types of interview. The questioning turned up decisions and examples my CV had flattened into one line.

This public guide is a portable version for you to try and adapt. It’s a method, not a promise. It hasn’t been independently tested, and it doesn’t guarantee interviews or offers.

Bear and both of Bear’s stories are fictional. The organisations are unnamed and the figures are invented for illustration.

Keep your latest approved files somewhere safe, and bring them to every new chat.
